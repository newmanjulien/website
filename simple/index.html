Go-to-market speed

It starts with simplifying

Elon Musk was quickly able to build more data centers faster than anyone else in the tech industry. He was also able to quickly build more cars faster than anyone else in the auto industry.

How has he been able to consistently achieve impossible speeds at the world's hardest projects?

According to Tesla's President, his speed start with simplifying (link).

The first thing Tesla did when starting to manufacture cars was to strip out parts and steps. They stripped out parts from the cars. Parts which in Elon's telling (link) people often didn't even know what the parts did. And they stripped out steps from their factories by challenging every single piece of the process.

It's this hard earned simplicity which laid the foundation for Elon's impossible speed. Simpler factories are exponentially faster to build, have exponentially less problems and run exponentially faster. Simpler cars are exponentially faster to build, get built with exponentially less problems and come off the assembly line exponentially faster. And both are infinitely repeatable and scalable.

Elon's principle applies to doing go-to-market with AI.

AI gives each of us our own factory which we build and operate to generate revenue.

We can run our factory like GM: slow, gummed up with unnecessary steps and hard to run. Or we can run our factory like Tesla: fast, smooth and simple.

I've been applying this approach to go-to-market with my own businesses. In the past, I created startups that had lots of complexity. We had VCs and engineers and sales teams. Today, I've been able to get more results faster with just me and Claude Code.

In fact, I've been able to build 2 businesses which have +$1m of ACV in under 1 year. I built both of them completely alone with $0 of funding.

What does simplicity mean for go-to-market?

First and foremost, it means baking go-to-market into your product or service. The simplest form of go-to-market is none at all.

My first business Overbase helps professional services firms find revenue by sharing data with partners. Using our product involves talking about our product to partners. Which means our main channel is no channel.

This first level of simplicity comes from designing your business. Which is a type of strategic thinking you might do before starting the business, but it's also strategy you need to work on actively as you execute. Simplifying go-to-market means always asking how you could sell without selling at all. There are some silver bullets. But it's mostly lots of lead bullets.

The second way to simplify is to pick 1 go-to-market channel. And only do that single channel.

My second business Dinu lets sales reps negotiate contracts themselves. It gets new customers with one single channel: cold email. We cold email leads to pitch our product. We cold email to invite them to join free consultations with lawyers. We do tons of different types of cold email. But if it's not cold email, we don't do it.

This second level of simplicity is mostly about removing things. Dinu for example has no social media presence. Anything that can be removed needs to be removed. Even if it seems like a no-brainer to keep it and even if it seems like a small removal that shouldn't matter. That's what's hard about simplifying. It always feels like it's not worth removing the thing.

The third way to simplify is to have less team. The ideal team is no team, but definitely your team needs to be small and flat.

The mantra in go-to-market during the SaaS era was specialization. You wanted SDRs, AEs, Onboarding specialists, CSMs, Renewals specialists, etc. You needed a CMO and a CCO, and the list of departments went on and on and on.

The mantra was also all about rinse-and-repeat processes that coin operated people could be successfully slotted in and out of.

The AI era in the opposite. We need a handful of generalists who get everything done on their own with 0 process. This is of course a huge advantage for net new businesses who don't have legacy teams and legacy processes. But I bet you it's also an advantage which any company leader could lean into.

The final point isn't so much about go-to-market specifically but rather about our broader approach.

As an individual who is trying to get things done in the current environment, we also need to simplify ourselves.

In our personal lives, this can mean cutting out our commute by moving within walking distance from your office. Or it can mean getting off social media. Or it can mean quitting the stock market. Or it can mean having less arguments with our spouse. For me personally, I don't have a cell phone anymore, I don't have internet at home and I don't have a TV. I also go to sleep when the sun sets and wake up when the sun rises.

At work, this is usually about removing tools from your stack. For me personally, I almost exclusively use Google Workspace. My CRM is Google Sheets and sometimes even just Google Tasks. I use Antigravity and I don't use non-coding agents like Hermes, Grok Bot or Muse. I'm reachable over email and I'm otherwise not reachable. Trying new tools can be good. But using them is almost never right.

Simplifying ourselves is hard but simplifying ourselves is a huge point of leverage in a business environment where each individual's contribution can be infinitely huge.

People often think of Cal Newport when I talk about simplifying. And I believe that's wrong. Cal Newport has way too many rules and processes. In my telling, he's a mess of complexity and overengineering. The purest form of simple I've come accross is Isaac Asimov who was one of the most prolific and successful authors of all time. His approach to writing was simple, he lived in a small house in the middle of nowhere and he'd write when there was nothing he'd rather do. Which was almost all the time.

I'll leave you on a final note and say that simplifying is likely the hardest possible thing anyone can do. There's a reason GM hasn't simplified after seeing how well it worked for Tesla. It's the same reason why some of these proposals may sound provocative or counter-cultural. And it's the same reason why your competitors won't follow if you seriously simplify your go-to-market.

A trend in VC right now is to say that speed is the new moat. And perhaps what they're really talking about is simplicity.
